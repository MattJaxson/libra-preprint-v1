\begin{abstract}
Fairness audits require choices about groups, reference populations, outcome
definitions, and decision thresholds. This paper presents \libra, a prototype
that stores some of those choices with process metadata and applies a selected
threshold and reference group in a group-outcome audit. We review its
implementation boundaries and perform descriptive sensitivity analyses; we do
not evaluate a participatory process or an AI model. No community participant
data are used.

In a 2024 single-lender Michigan HMDA extract retaining identical public-use
rows, the Black-to-White ratio for observed origination among all retained
action codes is $0.8174$ ($n=3{,}831$), but it is $0.7162$ when restricted to
originated versus denied applications ($n=2{,}925$). In a historical ProPublica COMPAS extract, the
African-American-to-Caucasian ratio for no recorded two-year recidivism is
$0.8009$ ($n=6{,}837$); this is an observed outcome, not a COMPAS prediction
error measure. A threshold change from $0.80$ to $0.85$ changes two selected
point-estimate labels under the original outcome definitions, while the HMDA
label also changes under a plausible outcome definition. Code review and new
tests show that non-demo finalization can now be constrained to its approved
draft and that a finalized reference group cannot be overridden in the core
racial-audit path. The signed receipt covers only selected fields, however,
and neither metadata completeness nor self-declared observer approval
establishes community authorization. The evidence supports a limited software
mechanism and a methodological warning, not a validated community standard.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} participatory AI; algorithmic auditing; disparate
impact; public-sector AI; provenance; community governance

\section{Introduction}

Algorithmic fairness is not reducible to selecting a single metric. Several
widely used fairness criteria are mutually incompatible except under restricted
conditions \cite{chouldechova2017,kleinberg2017}. Operational tools such as
AIF360, Aequitas, Fairlearn, and FairVis make measurement and mitigation more
accessible \cite{bellamy2018,saleiro2018,weerts2023,cabrera2019}, but a prior
governance question remains: who is authorized to choose the reference group,
the acceptable threshold, and the evidence sufficient to support a finding?

Participatory AI research has shown that affected people can contribute
substantive knowledge about system goals, harms, and trade-offs
\cite{lee2019,katell2020,birhane2022}. It has also warned that participation can
be extractive or symbolic when institutions retain control over scope and
implementation \cite{sloane2022}. Recent participatory-auditing studies further
show that non-specialists surface impacts absent from standard taxonomies, while
also needing scaffolding to translate lived concerns into measurable tests
\cite{zhao2026,dicampli2026,rezk2026,escher2026}.

This paper addresses a narrow systems problem within that larger literature:
how can proposed audit parameters be represented with their claimed source and
passed to an audit engine? We present \libra, a prototype interface. Its
contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a specification of a complete audit standard
  $C^*=(S,G,r,O,\theta,P,E)$, covering scope, groups, reference, outcome
  definition, threshold, process provenance, and evidence requirements, plus
  a gap analysis of what the current prototype binds;
  \item an implementation review and regression tests of two parameter-binding
  invariants, together with a disclosure of unverified authority and receipt
  coverage; and
  \item a reproducible sensitivity analysis showing the effect of both
  $\theta$ and the outcome definition on descriptive group ratios.
\end{enumerate}

The paper makes no claim that participatory auditing, provenance, registers, or
machine-readable AI governance are new categories. It reports no
community-defined threshold, completed civic pilot, government endorsement,
model-performance result, or improved social outcome. Its modest finding is
that analytic and governance choices can change a reported label; whether
\libra\ can legitimately translate community authority remains untested.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Fairness metrics and audit toolkits}

Group-fairness research provides multiple, sometimes incompatible, ways to
compare outcomes and errors \cite{chouldechova2017,kleinberg2017}. AIF360,
Aequitas, Fairlearn, and FairVis provide metrics, visualization, and mitigation
methods \cite{bellamy2018,saleiro2018,weerts2023,cabrera2019}. These systems are
valuable technical foundations; \libra's focus is not metric invention. Its
focus is the authority and provenance attached to the parameters supplied to an
audit.

The four-fifths rule is an employment-selection heuristic in the U.S. Uniform
Guidelines, not a universal legal definition of discrimination
\cite{eeoc1978}. In this paper, $0.80$ is used as a familiar comparison scenario.
Applying that number to lending or criminal-justice data is illustrative and
does not establish legal compliance in either domain.

LLM-bias studies target different evaluation objects. Pang et al. use the
acronym \emph{LIBRA} for a local-context LLM-bias framework
\cite{pang2025}; this project is unrelated and makes no claim to that name's
originality. Bouchard develops use-case-specific choices of LLM fairness
metrics \cite{bouchard2026}, and BiasFreeBench evaluates mitigation through
generated responses \cite{xu2026}. The present HMDA and COMPAS calculations
do not evaluate LLM outputs or extend those benchmarks.

\subsection{Participation and community-informed auditing}

WeBuildAI demonstrated collective participation in designing an algorithmic
policy for food-assistance allocation \cite{lee2019}. The Algorithmic Equity
Toolkit supported community advocates auditing government technologies
\cite{katell2020}. Birhane et al. synthesize opportunities and unresolved power
questions in participatory AI, while Sloane et al. caution that participation
alone is not a design fix \cite{birhane2022,sloane2022}.

The closest recent work substantially narrows any broad novelty claim. Zhao et
al. co-designed community-centered auditing tools with 22 educators in Hawai'i
and argue that affected communities should shape who audits, the infrastructure
used, and the desired outcomes \cite{zhao2026}. Di Campli San Vito et al. show
that non-expert stakeholders identify overlooked harms but require assistance
operationalizing them \cite{dicampli2026}. Rezk et al. find that participatory
auditors' trust in apparently capable systems can reduce scrutiny
\cite{rezk2026}. Escher et al. conduct a community-informed audit grounded in
Kyrgyz caregivers' concerns about language and platform recommendations
\cite{escher2026}. These studies provide empirical participation evidence that
the present paper does not.

\subsection{Provenance and operational governance artifacts}

The Participation Ledger proposes machine-readable records linking community
contributions to consent, compensation, authority, system changes, and
replayable tests \cite{mushkani2026ledger}. Its design substantially overlaps
\libra's provenance and participation ambitions; the present work does not
claim to originate those ideas or to outperform that proposal. Its narrower
implementation target is a fairness-audit configuration and its use at an
audit call. Participatory provenance measures
how public submissions survive AI-mediated consultation summaries
\cite{mahajan2026}. Voices in the Loop offers a versioned and contestable atlas
of participatory-AI initiatives \cite{mushkani2026voices}.

Adjacent public-sector work also emphasizes reviewable artifacts. Vendor
factsheets can support dialogue but are insufficient as standalone risk
assessments \cite{kuehnert2026}. Li proposes a minimum reviewable trace for
AI-influenced public decisions \cite{li2026}. The Eticas taxonomy maps risk
concepts into tests, measurements, severity bands, and JSON-LD infrastructure
\cite{galdon2026}. A global comparison of 8,368 public-sector AI-register
records finds limited convergence and frequent omissions involving appeals,
risks, legal bases, and external evaluation \cite{das2026}. \libra is positioned
as a narrower prototype: a community-process output becomes an explicit audit
input whose origin remains visible in the result.

\section{Framework}

\subsection{Audit parameters}

Let $D=\{(x_i,a_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be a dataset with sensitive-group label
$a_i\in A$ and binary favorable-outcome indicator $y_i\in\{0,1\}$. For group
$a$, the \emph{sample} favorable-outcome rate is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_a = \frac{\sum_i y_i\mathbb{1}[a_i=a]}
              {\sum_i \mathbb{1}[a_i=a]}.
\end{equation}
Given reference group $r$, the sample outcome-rate ratio is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat R(a,r)=\frac{\widehat p_a}{\widehat p_r},
  \qquad \widehat p_r>0.
\end{equation}
A deterministic threshold rule flags group $a$ when
$\widehat R(a,r)<\theta$. Equality does not trigger a flag.
If the reference rate is zero, the ratio is undefined rather than a pass.
These quantities describe the recorded sample; they do not estimate a causal
effect without additional assumptions. The implementation names this quantity
\code{disparate\_impact}, but for the COMPAS non-recidivism example it is only
an outcome-rate ratio, not an employment-selection disparate-impact finding.

An audit standard sufficient to interpret the ratio should be
\begin{equation}
  C^*=(S,G,r,O,\theta,P,E),
\end{equation}
where $S$ defines domain, jurisdiction, dataset eligibility, and other scope
constraints; $G\subseteq A$ contains priority groups; $O$ defines the outcome
column, favorable value, and included records; $P$ contains process provenance;
and $E$ contains evidence requirements such as minimum group size and acceptable
attestation sources. This is a design requirement, \emph{not} the current
implementation. The prototype stores some elements of $S,G,r,\theta,P,E$, but
does not bind $O$ to the finalized record, and it does not enforce all declared
scope and evidence conditions. Table~\ref{tab:config} summarizes the prototype
schema and its most important omission.

\subsection{Authority classification and lifecycle}

Three different claims must be separated: (i) a configuration has required
metadata, (ii) some fields are bound to a signed receipt, and (iii) the people
affected authorized a standard. The code's \code{community\_valid} label is
primarily a metadata-validation result; it is \emph{not} evidence for (iii).
For example, a demo configuration with zero recorded participants and otherwise
populated fields returns \code{is\_community\_valid=True} in the current helper.
This is a naming and governance-safety defect, not a community decision.
Similarly, \code{active\_standard} means a record passed a software lifecycle,
not that its participants were representative or authorized. An observer name
and action are submitted under the same tenant API key, so the current API
does not independently authenticate that observer.

The API supports draft records, finalization, active-standard resolution, and
receipts. In this revision, non-demo finalization rejects a request that
differs from the approved draft, and the core racial-audit path rejects an
attempt to override the finalized reference group. These are specific
software invariants, not a complete governance guarantee. The public registry
contains no verified community standard at the time of this analysis.

\subsection{Implementation}

The implementation consists of a Python package, a FastAPI service, a React
interface, a configuration format, and a registry. The group-rate engine
computes rates, ratios, threshold flags, and a max--min rate gap. Its historical
\code{community\_valid} output name should be read only as schema completeness.
The current signed ledger hash covers the council label, participant count,
demographic and consensus summaries, input protocol, threshold, priority
groups, reference group, and previous hash. It does \emph{not} cover the entire
configuration, including domain, jurisdiction, evidence requirements,
attestor policy, or process-integrity metadata. The ordinary racial-audit
response carries governance metadata but not an attached signed receipt.
The outcome column and favorable value are supplied at audit time rather than
bound by the finalized configuration. A caller can therefore change the
estimand while using the same threshold and reference group.
Consequently, this implementation does not yet prove end-to-end binding from
an authorized process through a complete standard to an individual audit.

For a concrete \emph{researcher-defined} trace, let $G$ contain Black or
African American HMDA records, $r$ be White, and $\theta=0.85$. With
\code{action\_taken=1} defined as favorable among all retained action codes,
the engine compares $0.8174$ with $0.85$ and flags the group. Changing only
$\theta$ to $0.80$ removes the flag. Holding $\theta=0.80$ but restricting the
outcome to originated versus denied records yields $0.7162$ and restores the
flag. This trace contains no community decision and shows why a future standard
must bind the outcome definition as well as its threshold.

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Research questions}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.6em,style=nextline]
  \item[RQ1] Which parameter-binding invariants hold in the tested
  implementation, and which provenance or authority claims remain unsupported?
  \item[RQ2] How do threshold and outcome-definition choices change descriptive
  group labels in the two extracts?
  \item[RQ3] Which analyses can be regenerated from the disclosed code and
  hashed local snapshots without participant data?
\end{description}

\subsection{Datasets}

\paragraph{HMDA Michigan.}
The local source file contains 4,463 records from one 2024 Michigan lender
filing \cite{cfpbHMDA}, identified by LEI \code{B4TYDEB6GKMZO031MB27}.
Records with unavailable, free-form, joint, multi-minority, or ``Other'' race
labels were excluded. The race filter leaves 3,831 records, including four rows
identical across released fields. Because identical public-use rows are not
proven duplicate real-world applications, the primary analysis retains them.
Exact-row deduplication is reported only as a sensitivity check; it leaves
3,827 records and changes the focal ratio from $0.8174$ to $0.8195$. The
favorable outcome is \code{action\_taken=1} (loan originated); all
other retained action codes are treated as non-origination in the original
scenario. The official coding distinguishes approved-but-not-accepted (2),
denied (3), withdrawn (4), and incomplete (5) from originated (1)
\cite{cfpbHMDAguide}. Therefore, non-origination is not synonymous with denial.
All 169 purchased-loan records (code 6) in the raw file have unavailable race
and are excluded by the race filter.
We also analyze (a) originated versus denied only, and (b) originated or
approved-but-not-accepted versus denied. These are different estimands, not
alternative estimates of the same one. The exact state/LEI/action query was
reconstructed from the official CFPB API \cite{cfpbAPI}. On September 30, 2026,
a fresh response had the same 4,463 parsed rows and 99 columns as the local
snapshot, including row multiplicities; its byte hash differed because row
order differed. The local byte hash and order-insensitive row-multiset digest
are supplied with acquisition steps.

\paragraph{COMPAS.}
We use 7,214 records in ProPublica's Broward County COMPAS release
\cite{angwin2016,propublicaData}. Excluding the ``Other'' label leaves 6,837 records. The
favorable outcome is no recorded two-year recidivism. Although the source file
contains COMPAS score fields, this analysis does not use them. Thus it does not
measure score error, predictive fairness, or a system decision. The dataset is
a contested historical benchmark used here only as a second descriptive
threshold example.

\subsection{Analysis}

We calculate group rates and ratios relative to White records in HMDA and
Caucasian records in COMPAS. These reference groups are analyst-selected for
the illustration, not community-authorized. We sweep
$\theta\in\{0.70,0.75,0.80,0.85,0.90,0.95\}$. Aggregate counts exclude groups
with fewer than 30 observations. The minimum is an analytic reporting rule,
not a validated power calculation or a rule enforced by every API response.
Small groups remain visible in the appendix without classification labels.

To show one component of uncertainty, the original analysis generates 10,000
stratified parametric bootstrap replicates with fixed seed 20,260,930. Within
each group, favorable counts are drawn from a binomial distribution using the
observed rate. The resulting percentile intervals condition on group and
outcome definitions and assume independent Bernoulli records. They do not
capture lender selection, administrative coding, dependence, measurement error,
or analytic-choice uncertainty. The outcome-definition analysis uses exact
descriptive counts rather than a second inferential model.

For RQ1, we inspected the non-demo draft/finalize and racial-audit reference
paths and added regression tests that attempt to finalize altered parameters
and override a finalized reference group. We also examined which fields are
included in the receipt hash and how observer approval is authenticated. This
is a focused implementation audit, not a formal security proof.

cutoff is better.

These are not adjusted comparisons: applicant mix, product type, location,
credit variables, and lender processes are uncontrolled.

\subsection{Implementation invariants and remaining gaps}

The new regression tests pass for two limited properties: an approved non-demo
draft cannot be finalized with a changed threshold, and a finalized racial
audit cannot silently switch its reference group through a caller override.
The inspection also exposes non-covered receipt fields and self-declared
observer approval, as described above. These prevent an end-to-end claim of
authenticated community control. The current experiment does not test an
actual process-to-audit trace with participant evidence.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the experiment establishes}

With a fixed ratio, changing $\theta$ changes its flag when the ratio lies
between two cutoffs. This is a software behavior check, not an empirical test
of \libra's social value. The HMDA sensitivity adds a less trivial lesson:
defining $Y$ is itself a substantive governance choice. A system that traces
only $\theta$ and $r$ but not the outcome definition still leaves a major source
of analytic discretion outside the claimed standard.

Reports should identify whether a threshold, reference group, and outcome
definition came from law, an auditor default, a vendor, a research scenario,
or a documented process. These sources cannot be collapsed into a generic
``community'' label. The actual audit record must also bind to the complete
approved standard before an enforcement claim is justified.

\subsection{What the experiment does not establish}

It does not establish that a community prefers $0.85$ or $0.90$, that
participants understand the parameterization, that the facilitation protocol
works, or that an institution responds. Neither dataset result is a measured
AI-system output disparity. The ratios are not legal conclusions. The two
regression tests do not verify observer identity, complete receipt binding,
consent, representativeness, or deployment security.

\subsection{Design implications for prospective fieldwork}

Recent work suggests that a field study should go beyond asking participants to
move a numeric slider. Participants should help define harms, evidence sources,
perspectives, acceptable outcomes, and the authority to pause or reject a
system \cite{zhao2026,dicampli2026,mushkani2026ledger}. The process should record
disagreement instead of compressing it into false consensus, compensate
participants, report whose perspectives are absent, and specify who owns and
maintains the resulting artifact. ``Do not use this system'' must remain a valid
outcome. Otherwise, machine-readable participation can become participation
washing with better metadata.

\section{Limitations and Ethics}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No participant study.} No residents, educators, public-sector
  staff, or affected decision subjects supplied data for this paper. No current
  configuration is described as community-defined.
  \item \textbf{Descriptive, non-causal analysis.} Group outcome ratios do not
  identify why disparities occur and cannot establish unlawful discrimination,
  model bias, or institutional intent.
  \item \textbf{Dataset scope and outcomes.} The HMDA file covers one lender;
  its original binary outcome mixes denial with withdrawal, incompleteness,
  and approved-but-not-accepted actions. The restricted outcomes substantially
  change the focal ratio. COMPAS is geographically and historically specific;
  this study does not use its model scores. Neither case supports an AI-model
  or population-generalization claim.
  \item \textbf{Categorization.} Administrative race labels flatten identity
  and omit intersectional structure. Some groups remain too small for stable
  interpretation; a minimum-$n$ rule reduces but does not solve this problem.
  \item \textbf{Uncertainty.} The bootstrap intervals cross decision
  thresholds and rely on a conditional Bernoulli model. A mature implementation
  should support evidentiary review rather than relying on point-estimate flags.
  \item \textbf{Governance validity and binding.} Provenance fields cannot
  establish representativeness, independence, consent, or influence. The
  observer endpoint does not independently authenticate a reviewer; the signed
  receipt covers selected configuration fields and is not attached to the
  ordinary racial-audit response. External review and further engineering are
  needed before a strong governance claim.
  \item \textbf{Prototype maturity.} Passing tests does not establish
  production security or availability. The public deployment claim from an
  earlier draft was not verified for this paper and is intentionally omitted.
\end{itemize}

Any future study that collects participant responses for publication should
receive an institutional ethics or IRB determination before recruitment. It
should use informed consent, privacy minimization, withdrawal procedures,
participant compensation, and explicit authorization for quotations and
artifact publication. District 3 and the City of Detroit are prospective
contexts only; neither is represented as an endorser, adopter, or completed
pilot in this paper.

\section{Conclusion}

\libra\ is a prototype for recording audit-parameter source and applying a
configured threshold and reference group. The descriptive cases show both
threshold and outcome-definition sensitivity; the latter reverses the original
HMDA threshold story under reasonable restrictions. Focused tests establish
two parameter-binding invariants, while receipt scope and authority remain
incomplete. The project is therefore an inspectable prototype, not yet evidence
that affected communities govern an AI audit. The larger claim needs a
complete, verifiable process-to-audit trace and prospective, ethically reviewed
fieldwork.
